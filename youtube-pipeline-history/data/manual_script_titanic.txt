On the night of April 14th, 1912, the largest moving object ever built by human hands was tearing through the freezing North Atlantic at nearly full speed, and almost nobody on board believed anything could stop it.

The RMS Titanic was over 882 feet long, weighed 46,000 tons, and had been called unsinkable in the press before it had even left port. That word would haunt the ship's legacy forever.
[VISUAL: Titanic construction Belfast shipyard]

Titanic was built by the Harland and Wolff shipyard in Belfast for the White Star Line, designed to outclass its rival Cunard not with speed, but with sheer size and luxury.
[VISUAL: Titanic first class dining room interior]

It had a gymnasium, a heated swimming pool, a Turkish bath, and a first-class dining saloon that could seat more than five hundred passengers at once.
[VISUAL: Titanic swimming pool 1912]

But beneath the luxury was a fatal design compromise. Titanic's hull was divided into sixteen watertight compartments, and shipbuilders believed the vessel could stay afloat even if four of them flooded.
[VISUAL: Titanic watertight compartments diagram]

Nobody planned for six.

On April 10th, 1912, Titanic departed Southampton, England, on its maiden voyage to New York City, carrying an estimated two thousand two hundred passengers and crew.
[VISUAL: Southampton docks 1912 crowd]

Among them were some of the wealthiest people in the world, including businessman John Jacob Astor the Fourth, one of the richest men alive at the time.
[VISUAL: John Jacob Astor IV portrait]

But the ship also carried hundreds of emigrants in third class, families seeking a new life in America, packed into far more crowded and far less safe conditions below deck.
[VISUAL: Titanic third class passengers 1912]

For four days, the crossing was smooth. Then, on the evening of April 14th, the temperature began dropping sharply, a warning sign of icebergs nearby that the crew did not fully heed.
[VISUAL: North Atlantic ice field 1912]

Titanic had received at least six iceberg warnings from other ships that day. Most were acknowledged. Not all of them reached the bridge in time.
[VISUAL: Titanic wireless radio room]

At 11:40 PM, lookout Frederick Fleet spotted a dark shape looming out of the darkness directly ahead. He rang the warning bell three times and telephoned the bridge: iceberg, right ahead.
[VISUAL: Titanic lookout crows nest]

The ship's First Officer ordered the engines reversed and the wheel turned hard, but at over twenty knots, there was no time. Titanic scraped along the iceberg's underwater ice shelf for about ten seconds.
[VISUAL: iceberg North Atlantic photograph]

It didn't look catastrophic from the deck. Some passengers reported barely feeling a jolt. Below the waterline, it was already over.
[VISUAL: Titanic hull cross section diagram]

The collision had popped rivets and buckled steel plates along roughly three hundred feet of the hull, opening the sea to five, and possibly six, of the sixteen watertight compartments.
[VISUAL: Titanic damaged hull illustration]

Titanic's chief designer, Thomas Andrews, was aboard for the maiden voyage. Within minutes of inspecting the damage, he delivered the verdict privately to Captain Edward Smith: the ship was doomed.
[VISUAL: Thomas Andrews Titanic designer portrait]

Andrews calculated the ship had perhaps two hours left. He was almost exactly right.
[VISUAL: Captain Edward Smith Titanic]

At 12:05 AM, Captain Smith gave the order to uncover the lifeboats and begin loading women and children first. But Titanic carried only twenty lifeboats, enough for barely half of everyone on board.
[VISUAL: Titanic lifeboats deck 1912]

The ship technically exceeded the outdated safety regulations of the time, which based lifeboat requirements on a vessel's tonnage, not its actual passenger capacity.
[VISUAL: Titanic lifeboat regulations 1912 newspaper]

Many of the early lifeboats were lowered only partially full. Passengers didn't believe the ship, the largest and most advanced in the world, could actually be sinking.
[VISUAL: Titanic lifeboat launching photograph]

As the night wore on and the ship's bow sank lower into the water, the truth became impossible to deny. Panic began to spread across the decks.
[VISUAL: Titanic sinking deck angle illustration]

The ship's band, led by violinist Wallace Hartley, famously continued playing music on deck as the situation grew more desperate, an act that became one of the most remembered details of the disaster.
[VISUAL: Titanic ship band musicians 1912]

Just after 2 AM, the lights flickered, failed, and Titanic's stern rose high into the air as the ship began to break apart under its own weight.
[VISUAL: Titanic sinking stern rising illustration]

At 2:20 AM on April 15th, 1912, RMS Titanic disappeared beneath the surface of the North Atlantic, roughly two hours and forty minutes after striking the iceberg.
[VISUAL: Titanic final plunge illustration]

More than 1,500 people died in the frigid water, most from hypothermia within minutes, not drowning. The water temperature that night was close to 28 degrees Fahrenheit.
[VISUAL: North Atlantic Ocean cold water]

The RMS Carpathia, roughly fifty-eight miles away, raced through the same ice field to reach the scene, arriving just after 4 AM to begin rescuing the roughly 705 survivors from the lifeboats.
[VISUAL: RMS Carpathia rescue ship 1912]

The disaster triggered sweeping changes to maritime law. The International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea was established in 1914, mandating lifeboat space for every person aboard a ship.

The International Ice Patrol was also created, a monitoring system that still tracks iceberg danger in North Atlantic shipping lanes to this day.
[VISUAL: International Ice Patrol iceberg monitoring]

For seventy-three years, Titanic's exact resting place remained unknown, sitting more than twelve thousand feet below the surface in near-total darkness.
[VISUAL: deep ocean darkness North Atlantic]

In September 1985, a joint American and French expedition led by oceanographer Robert Ballard finally located the wreck, split into two main sections nearly half a mile apart on the ocean floor.
[VISUAL: Titanic wreck discovery 1985 Robert Ballard]

Today, Titanic remains one of the most studied shipwrecks in history, a story that endures not just because of its scale, but because of how avoidable so much of the tragedy actually was.
